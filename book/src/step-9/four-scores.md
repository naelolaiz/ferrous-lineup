# Four pieces from their scores: a zamba, a song, an aria and a chorus

Files: [`crates/lineup-core/src/library/song/classical.rs`](https://github.com/naelolaiz/ferrous-lineup/blob/main/crates/lineup-core/src/library/song/classical.rs),
[`crates/lineup-core/src/library/song/folklore_arranged.rs`](https://github.com/naelolaiz/ferrous-lineup/blob/main/crates/lineup-core/src/library/song/folklore_arranged.rs),
[`crates/lineup-core/src/library/song.rs`](https://github.com/naelolaiz/ferrous-lineup/blob/main/crates/lineup-core/src/library/song.rs)
and [`crates/lineup-core/src/pattern.rs`](https://github.com/naelolaiz/ferrous-lineup/blob/main/crates/lineup-core/src/pattern.rs)

After the [Misa Criolla](misa-criolla.md), four more demos were written
out note for note from their scores, every voice and instrument on a
channel of its own:

| Demo | What it is | Meter and key | Who plays |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alfonsina y el mar** | Ariel Ramírez and Félix Luna's zamba, 1969 | 6/8, C minor | a choir in four parts |
| **Die Forelle** | Franz Schubert's song "The Trout", D 550, 1817 | 2/4, D flat major | voice and piano |
| **Sheep May Safely Graze** | an aria from Johann Sebastian Bach's Hunting Cantata, BWV 208, 1713 | 4/4, B flat major | soprano, two recorders, continuo |
| **For Unto Us a Child Is Born** | a chorus from George Frideric Handel's Messiah, 1741 | 4/4, G major | choir, strings, oboes, continuo |

As in the Misa Criolla, the voices sing without the words.

## Trying it

1. Open **File > Demos**, search `Forelle` and press Play. Watch the
   Keys channel's piano roll: the piano's right hand jumps up in quick
   groups of six, and the left hand plays a bass note and then a chord.
2. Open **Sheep May Safely Graze** and solo the two **Recorder**
   channels. They play in thirds, and each phrase is echoed, first
   forte and then piano.
3. Open **For Unto Us a Child Is Born** and open the **mixer**: Lead
   (the sopranos), Altos, Tenors, Basses, Violins I, Violins II, Violas,
   Oboes and Keys (the bassi, played by cellos). Mute everything but
   the four voices and listen to them come in one after another: the
   sopranos in bar 7, the tenors in bar 12, the altos in bar 18 and the
   basses in bar 19.
4. In the same demo, watch the key in the harmony bar: G major, D major
   from bar 18, G again from bar 38, C major from bar 56, and G from bar
   71 to the end.
5. Open **Alfonsina y el mar** and look at the ruler: the very first bar
   is one eighth note long. It holds the upbeat that the song starts on.

## The music

### Alfonsina y el mar: a zamba in C minor

The **zamba** is the slow handkerchief dance of north-west Argentina,
in 6/8: two beats to the bar, each split in three. Ramírez and Luna
wrote this one for Mercedes Sosa, about the poet Alfonsina Storni. The
demo is sung by a choir in four parts. The arrangement writes out the
first half of the song: the introduction, two verses and the chorus.
The second half sings two more verses to the same music, so the demo
sings it all twice.

Three moments to listen for:

- The introduction rises from **F sharp to G** before it falls down the
  C minor chord. F sharp is not in C minor. It is the **lower neighbour**
  of G, a note a semitone below that leans into it.
- Each verse turns to **E flat major**, the **relative major** of C
  minor (the major key with the same key signature), through **B flat
  seventh** and then **B flat augmented** (B flat, D, F sharp). The
  augmented chord's F sharp rises to G, the third of E flat.
- The chorus reaches **F minor**, iv, through **G half-diminished** and
  **C seventh**: ii and V of F minor, as if F minor were the key for a
  moment. It ends on **D major** and **G seventh**. Their A natural and B natural
  are the notes of the **melodic minor** going up, and they climb to C.

### Die Forelle: a song in which the water changes

In Schubert's song a trout darts about in a clear brook, and a
fisherman muddies the water to catch it. The piano is the brook: the
right hand darts up in **sextuplets**, six sixteenths in the time of
four (D flat, F, G flat, G natural, A flat), and lands on an accented
eighth.

The first two verses have the same music. That makes the song
**strophic**: verses sung to the same tune, like a hymn. The harmony is
almost all **I and V7**, D flat and A flat seventh. In the middle of each
verse the music cadences in A flat, through **E flat seventh**: the
dominant of the dominant, V7/V. A chord that makes another chord sound
like a new tonic for a moment **tonicises** it. The last lines bring in
**IV**, G flat, and are sung twice.

The third verse is where the water turns muddy. The music changes, so
the song is **modified strophic**:

- **B flat minor**, vi, comes after its own dominant, F seventh;
- four bars of **E flat seventh** follow, in staccato chords under
  repeated D flats;
- then a chain of dominants goes round the **circle of fifths**: B flat
  seventh, E flat seventh, A flat seventh, and back to D flat. Each
  chord is the dominant of the next.

Then the last lines come back and the song ends as the first verse did.

### Sheep May Safely Graze: an aria in three parts

Bach wrote the Hunting Cantata for a duke's birthday. In this aria
Pales, the goddess of shepherds, sings over two recorders and the
**continuo**: the bass line, here played by a cello.

The aria is a **da capo aria**: a first part (A), a contrasting middle
(B), and then the first part again (A). "Da capo" means "from the
head". The demo plays it that way: Ritornello, the voice's two sections
and the Ritornello again, then the middle, then all of the first part
again, ending on the first beat of the middle's first bar under a
fermata.

- The opening **ritornello**, the instruments' refrain, sits on a held B
  flat in the bass: first I, then **V7 over the tonic's bass**. A bass
  note held while the chords change above it is a **pedal**.
- The middle section moves away from B flat major. It goes to **G
  minor** (vi, the relative minor), to **C minor** (ii), and to **F
  major** (V), turning through D minor and G minor on the way.
- The voice's three **trills**, rapid alternations with the note above,
  are written out as thirty-second notes.

### For Unto Us a Child Is Born: counterpoint, then block chords

Handel took the tune of this chorus from an Italian duet he had
written a few weeks before Messiah. The texture shows: the voices come
in one by one with the same subject, the same tune started on another
voice, and run in sixteenths. This is **imitation**, the technique of
a fugue. The continuo walks in eighths underneath.

Four times the whole choir stops running and calls out together, in
**block chords**, every voice on the same rhythm, forte, over the
violins' quick figures. These are the "Wonderful" passages. Each one is
in a different key, and together they show the chorus's **key plan**:

| Bars | Key | Seen from G |
|---|---|---|
| 33–37 | D major | V, the dominant |
| 49–53 | G major | I, the tonic |
| 68–72 | C major | IV, the subdominant |
| 85–91 | G major | I again |

Three more things to listen for:

- **A circle-of-fifths sequence.** In bars 41 and 42 the bass goes B, E,
  A, D, G, C, each note a fifth below the last (or a fourth above),
  under B minor, E minor, A minor, D, G and C. The roots move as in the
  third verse of Die Forelle, but here every chord belongs to G major:
  iii, vi, ii, V, I, IV.
- **The pivot to C.** In bar 56 an F major chord (F natural, not F
  sharp) turns the music to C major. The bass holds its F while the
  chord above becomes C, and then steps down to E: C over F, then C over
  E. Bar 73 does the same over C: G over C, then G over B. A bass note
  held into the next chord and then resolved down a step is a **bass
  suspension**.
- **The leading-tone chord.** In bars 26 and 27 an E in the bass is
  figured with a raised sixth (see below): the chord over it is E, G and
  C sharp, C sharp diminished over E, **vii°6** in D major. It rises to
  D over F sharp, **I6**.

### Figured bass: chords written as numbers

Baroque composers did not write the continuo's chords out. Under the
bass line they wrote small numbers, the **figures**, and the
keyboard player filled in the chords from them. Each figure is an
interval above the bass note. Friedrich Chrysander's edition of Messiah
prints figures over some of the bass, and the demo's chord chart
follows them where they are given:

| Figure | Means | In this chorus |
|---|---|---|
| none | a root-position triad | most bars |
| 6 | a triad in first inversion: the third in the bass | bar 1: B with 6 is G over B |
| 6/5 | a seventh chord in first inversion | bar 1: F sharp with 6/5 is D7 over F sharp |
| 4/2 | a seventh chord in third inversion: the seventh in the bass | bar 28: G with 4/2 is A7 over G; bar 43: C with 4/2 is D7 over C |
| 6 with a slash | first inversion, with the sixth raised a semitone | bar 26: E with a raised 6 is C sharp diminished over E |
| + (on its own) | the third raised | bar 22: A with C sharp, A major |
| a natural sign | the third made natural | bar 58: D with F natural, D minor |

A seventh chord in third inversion has its seventh in the bass, and a
seventh wants to fall a step. So a 4/2 chord is almost always followed
by a 6 on the note below. A7 over G goes to D over F sharp in bar 29.
D7 over C goes to G over B in bar 44.

## How it works

### From an engraving to a demo

Three of the four scores are engravings from the Mutopia Project,
written in **LilyPond**, a language for printing music as text. Here
are the first notes of Handel's first violins:

```text
re'''4 sol''8 sol'' do''' do''' do''' do''' |
```

The note names are Italian (`re` is D, `sol` is G, `do` is C). Each
`'` raises the note an octave, and the number is the length (`4` a
quarter, `8` an eighth). LilyPond can write a score as a MIDI file as
well as a page. A script read that file bar by bar and wrote the
demo's parts, as it did for the Misa Criolla:

- **Dynamics.** LilyPond plays each dynamic at a set velocity (*p* 69,
  *mf* 86, *f* 95), so the script reads those velocities back as the
  marks.
- **Staccato.** LilyPond plays a staccato note half as long as it is
  written. To tell a staccato quarter from an eighth, the score was
  compiled twice, once without the staccato dots. A note that is
  shorter in the first file is staccato, and it gets a `.` in the demo.
- **Thirty-second notes.** Bach's written-out trills are faster than
  the app's sixteenth-note grid. The notation can write half a step,
  `D5:1/2`, so the script writes them that way.

Some scores leave things out, and the demo comment above each song
says what was added. Sheep May Safely Graze marks only the recorders'
piano echoes, so the phrases before them are forte. Messiah's edition
prints its fortes in the first violins only and gives the voices no
dynamics, so the forte holds for all the strings and the choir sings
forte in each "Wonderful".

Editions have mistakes, too. Die Forelle's engraving writes D natural
in bars 43 and 44, against a D flat in the left hand. Bars 19 and 20
have the same music with D flat, and a second edition, OpenScore's
Lieder collection, has D flat there too, so the demo plays D flat.

### Bars of one eighth and one quarter

Alfonsina y el mar starts on an upbeat, one eighth note before the
first full bar. A demo part can have its own time signature, so the
upbeat is a part of its own, one bar of 1/8:

```rust
// A bar of one eighth note: the upbeat of a song in 6/8 (Alfonsina y el mar).
const ONE_EIGHT: TimeSignature = TimeSignature::new(1, 8);

Part::new("Pickup", "!mf F#4:2", "")
    .in_time(ONE_EIGHT)
```

`TimeSignature::new` is a `const fn`, a function Rust can run while it
compiles, so the time signature can be a `const`. Sheep May Safely
Graze uses the same idea at its end: the da capo stops on the first
beat of bar 21, so the last part is one bar of 1/4 with a fermata.

### Why each voice of the chorus has its own channel

The first version of the Handel demo had the altos, tenors and basses
on one Choir channel, as the Misa Criolla does. The playback trace
showed 11 notes missing, some of them held notes.

Two voices often sing the same note. In bar 75 the altos start an E in
sixteenths while the tenors hold an E for a whole beat. On one channel
those are two notes with the same pitch on the same step, and a
pattern never keeps two such notes:

```rust
/// Whether the two notes have the same pitch and start at the same
/// moment. A pattern never holds two such notes: they would be one
/// note played twice at once.
pub fn same_place(&self, other: &NoteEvent) -> bool {
    self.pitch == other.pitch && self.start == other.start && self.nudge == other.nudge
}

pub fn tidy_notes(notes: impl IntoIterator<Item = NoteEvent>) -> Vec<NoteEvent> {
    let mut tidy: Vec<NoteEvent> = Vec::new();
    for note in notes.into_iter().filter_map(NoteEvent::fitted) {
        let duplicate = tidy.iter().any(|other| other.same_place(&note));
        if !duplicate {
            tidy.push(note);
        }
    }
    tidy
}
```

`filter_map` runs `NoteEvent::fitted` on every note, keeping the
`Some` results and dropping the `None` ones (notes that do not fit in
the pattern). `any` stops at the first earlier note in the same place.
The first of the two wins: the altos' sixteenth was kept, and the
tenors' held E was lost.

The rule is right for a pattern: a piano roll cannot show two
identical notes on top of each other. The fix was in the demo. The
altos, tenors and basses became three lines with three channels, as
they would be on three staves of a score. The two oboes are different:
they play one line, often in unison, and where they play the same
note for the same length the script writes it once.

## Checking it

Each demo was played with the [playback trace](playback-trace.md) and
compared with its score's MIDI file, note by note: the start step, the
pitch and the channel of every note, and its length.

- **For Unto Us a Child Is Born:** all 3975 notes match. The only
  lengths that differ are the 14 staccato notes of bar 37, half as long
  as written.
- **Sheep May Safely Graze:** all 1646 notes and lengths match, through
  the da capo.
- **Die Forelle:** 1452 of 1460 notes match. Two are the D flats of
  bars 43 and 44. The other six are E flats that the right hand doubles
  in unison with the left hand's chord in bar 66: both hands are on the
  Keys channel, so each sounds once.
- **Alfonsina y el mar** was checked in both of its halves against the
  arrangement, with the swapped voices on the right channels.

2. In For Unto Us a Child Is Born, play bars 41 and 42 with only Keys

4. In Sheep May Safely Graze, find the middle section's bars in C
   minor on the chord sheet. Which chord brings in B natural, and why
   does C minor need it?

   returns how many would be dropped by `tidy_notes`, using `iter`,
   `enumerate` and `any`.
