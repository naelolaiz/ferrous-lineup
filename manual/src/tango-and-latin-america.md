# Tango and Latin America

One rhythm runs through this chapter. The Cuban **habanera** goes long,
short, long, long. Tie its two middle notes together and it becomes the
**tresillo**, three notes spread 3 + 3 + 2 (see [Rhythm and
meter](rhythm-and-meter.md#strong-beats-weak-beats-and-syncopation)).
In the 19th century it crossed to Spain, to Brazil and to the River
Plate, and each place made its own music on it. The demos are in
**Songs > Tango**, **Latin** and **Flamenco**.

## The habanera

- *La Paloma*, by Sebastián Iradier, written about 1860 after a visit
  to Cuba, went round the world. Only two chords, C and G7: the tune
  hangs on F, the seventh of G7, for a whole bar before each cadence.
- *Habanera*, the aria from Bizet's opera *Carmen* (1875), slides down
  one semitone at a time over the habanera rhythm in D minor, then turns
  to D major for its refrain.

## Tango

The tango grew up in Buenos Aires and Montevideo around 1900, out of
the habanera, the **milonga** and the Spanish tango. Most tangos are in
a minor key, and the dominant seventh, E7 in A minor, whose G♯ comes
from the harmonic minor, gives them their drama. Listen to the piano and
the bass: in the **marcato** style they hit every beat, short and hard,
while the tune pulls between long notes and quick runs.

![The chord sheet of the demo El Choclo, in A harmonic minor: bars 1 to 20 with Am and E7 (i, V7), A7 (V7/iv, in yellow) leading to Dm (iv), the second strain on G7 and C (♭VII7, ♭III), and at bar 18 "→ A major", A and E7 (I, V7), then F#7 (V7/ii, in yellow) to Bm (ii)](images/el-choclo.png)

*El Choclo*, by Ángel Villoldo (1903), is the oldest tango still played
everywhere, and its chord sheet holds a whole lesson. The first strain
runs up and down A minor and E7; A7, a secondary dominant, leads to D
minor; the second strain leans on the relative major, C, through its
own dominant, G7; and the third turns to A major, the tonic major, and
walks round the circle of fifths, F♯7, B minor, B7, E7. Villoldo's
tangos were still half milonga, so the bass plays the 3 + 3 + 2.

More to hear:

| Demo | Listen for |
|---|---|
| *La Cumparsita* (Gerardo Matos Rodríguez, 1916) | a first strain made of one two-bar idea four times, D7 answered by G minor, with the C♯ leaning into every D |
| *Por una cabeza* (Carlos Gardel, 1935) | quick chromatic turns in D major; the second strain in D minor, one rising figure three times, each a third lower |
| *Volver* (Carlos Gardel, 1934) | a verse in G minor that repeats a note, then the note a step below; a refrain in G major that borrows an E♭ chord from G minor |
| *Adiós muchachos* (Julio César Sanders, 1927) | B♭ minor, a chord borrowed from F minor, darkening F major |
| *Caminito* (Juan de Dios Filiberto, 1926) | an introduction in A major, a verse in A minor and a refrain back in A major |
| *Garúa* (Aníbal Troilo, 1943) | a bass walking down under A7, and B♭7 sliding down a half step to A7 |
| *Milonga del río* (written for Ferrous Lineup) | the milonga, quicker and happier than the tango: the tune itself plays the 3 + 3 + 2 |

The **vals criollo** is the tango's waltz. *Desde el alma*, by Rosita
Melo (1911), starts on the dominant, F♯7, and only reaches B minor in
its third bar.

## Brazil

In Rio de Janeiro the habanera and the polka became the **maxixe**, the
**tango brasileiro** and the **choro**, the instrumental music of the
city around 1900, for flute, guitar and *cavaquinho*. These are in 2/4,
written here two bars to one bar of 4/4.

- *Tico-Tico no Fubá*, by Zequinha de Abreu (1917): a rondo, A A B B A
  C C A, with A in A minor, B in its relative major, C, and C in its
  parallel major, A.
- *Apanhei-te, cavaquinho*, by Ernesto Nazareth (1914): runs down G major
  in sixteenths, each beat leaning on the semitone below it.
- *Carioca*, by Nazareth (1913): a tango brasileiro whose first strain
  cadences through the Neapolitan chord.
- *Atraente*, a polka by Chiquinha Gonzaga (1877), and her *Ó abre
  alas* (1899), counted the first carnival march.

Sixty years later, the **bossa nova** slowed the samba down and filled
it with jazz chords. *Garota de Ipanema*, by Antônio Carlos Jobim and
Vinícius de Moraes (1962), surprises at its second chord: E♭7, the
dominant of the dominant, where E♭ minor 7 would be expected. Its bridge
jumps up a half step, to D major, and comes home through a long chain of
chords.

## Cuba and Mexico

- *El manisero*, by Moisés Simons (1928): the peanut seller's cry made
  into a Cuban **son**, the first Cuban hit around the world. The clave
  takes two bars, and the bass plays no note on the first beat: it plays
  on the "and" of 2 and on 4, where it already plays the root of the
  next chord.
- **Boleros**, the slow love songs of Cuba and Mexico: *Quiéreme mucho*,
  *Aquellos ojos verdes*, *María Elena*, and Lecuona's *Siboney*, in F
  minor, whose C7 has a flat ninth.

## Flamenco

Three demos show the harmony of Spanish flamenco:

- *Rumba del sur*: the **Andalusian cadence**, Am, G, F, E, under a
  sung line, each line a step lower than the one before.
- *Noche frigia*: the **Phrygian mode** of the Spanish guitar, E F G A
  B C D, with a major chord on E; F major, a semitone above home, keeps
  pulling back down to E (see [Keys and modes](keys-and-modes.md#the-modes)).
- *Asturias (Leyenda)*, by Isaac Albéniz (1892), written for piano in
  imitation of the flamenco guitar: every other sixteenth note is the
  same B, a **pedal**, while the tune moves under it.

The Andalusian cadence turns up far from Spain, too: in *Hit the Road
Jack*, by Percy Mayfield, and in *La Llorona* from Mexico.
