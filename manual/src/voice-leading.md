# Voice leading and how parts move

The chord names say which notes sound together. **Voice leading** is
how each part gets from its note in one chord to its note in the next.
A **part**, or **voice**, is one line of notes: a singer, an instrument,
or one line inside a pianist's chords. Two pieces can use the same
chords and sound very different, smooth or clumsy, because their parts
move differently.

Harmony courses teach it with **four-part writing**: soprano, alto,
tenor and bass, as a choir sings a hymn or as Bach wrote his chorales.
A few rules keep each of the four a line of its own. The piano roll can
check them for you, and show how each part moves, as a teacher would
mark an exercise.

## Checking an exercise

Tick **Voice leading** next to **Ghost notes** at the top of the piano
roll. The piano roll then shows:

- **A Voices row** under the chord names, with a block over each chord
  where something classical part writing avoids happens: **5ths** and
  **8ves** for parallel fifths and octaves, **1sns** for parallel
  unisons, **LT** for a leading tone that doesn't rise, and **7th** for
  a seventh that doesn't fall.
- **A line of colour for each part**, from each of its notes to the
  next, so you can follow one part through the pattern as on a score.
  The moves into the chord you point at are drawn bolder.
- **A dashed outline** where a leading tone or a seventh should have
  gone.
- **A line over the chord names** that sums up the check, as in "Voice
  leading, 4 parts: parallel fifths (1), seventh not falling (1)."
  Pointing at a chord with a finding says what happens there instead.

Point at a block in the Voices row to read why part writing avoids it:

![Four parts in C major in the piano roll, C, F, G7 and C, a beat each, with Voice leading ticked. The Voices row marks 5ths over F and 7th over the last C. A line of colour joins the notes of each part, and a dashed outline on E4 shows where the seventh should have gone. The pointer is on the 5ths block: "Parallel fifths: Lineup Osc (voice 3 of 4) and Lineup Osc (lowest voice) go from G3 over C3 (a fifth) to C4 over F3 (a fifth), both up. Two parts a fifth apart that move the same way into another fifth blend into one sound and stop being heard as two lines, so classical part writing avoids them. Keep a note both chords share, or move the parts the opposite way."](images/voice-leading.png)

To write this exercise yourself, add an empty pattern, open the piano
roll, and draw these notes a beat each, the bass first:

| Part | C | F | G7 | C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| soprano | C5 | A4 | F4 | G4 |
| alto | E4 | F4 | D4 | E4 |
| tenor | G3 | C4 | B3 | C4 |
| bass | C3 | F3 | G3 | C3 |

It has two slips, on purpose. From C to F, the tenor and the bass are a
fifth apart and both rise a fourth into another fifth. From G7 to C,
the soprano's F, the seventh of G7, rises to G instead of falling to E.

Here is the same progression written well:

| Part | C | F | G7 | C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| soprano | C5 | C5 | G4 | G4 |
| alto | E4 | F4 | F4 | E4 |
| tenor | G3 | A3 | B3 | C4 |
| bass | C3 | F3 | G3 | C3 |

The line now reads "Voice leading, 4 parts: nothing classical part
writing avoids." Look at what changed: wherever two chords share a
note, a part keeps it (C5 from C to F, F4 from F to G7, G4 from G7 to
C), and the other parts move by step. The seventh, F4, falls to E4, and
the leading tone, B3, rises to C4. That is most of the craft.

## Which notes make a part

A track that plays one note at a time is one part. A track that plays
chords holds several: the top note of each chord goes to the top part,
the next one to the next part, and so on. The messages call them by
place, "voice 3 of 4" or "lowest voice", as in the picture, where all
four parts are on one track.

With **Ghost notes** on, the other tracks are checked too, each by its
name, so the bass can be on a track of its own. Drum tracks are left
out. A rest of a beat or more ends a line. Two parts that play in
octaves all the time, as basses doubling the cellos, are one line made
louder, and are counted once.

## The rules

### No parallel fifths or octaves

Two parts a perfect fifth apart that both move the same way into
another perfect fifth make **parallel fifths**. The same goes for
octaves and unisons. These intervals blend so well that the two parts
sound like one for that moment, and the music loses a line. A fifth
plus an octave still counts as a fifth.

Moving the opposite way from a fifth to a twelfth makes **fifths by
contrary motion**. Each part moves differently, but the ear still hears
two fifths in a row, so strict part writing avoids those too. A held or
repeated note doesn't count: both parts have to move.

### The leading tone rises

The **leading tone** is the note a semitone below the tonic: B in C
major, G♯ in A minor. When a dominant chord (V, V7, vii°) goes to the
tonic, the leading tone rises to the tonic. The rule holds in the top
part and the bass, where the ear follows the line. An inner part may
drop to the fifth of the tonic chord instead, so that the chord gets
all its notes, and the check leaves inner parts alone.

The check also expects the leading tone to rise in a **deceptive
cadence**, V to vi, and in a **secondary dominant**: in D7 to G, in C
major, the F♯ rises to G (see [Chords from outside the
key](outside-the-key.md#secondary-dominants)). Classical part writing
never doubles the leading tone, and the message says so when two parts
have it.

### A seventh falls

In a seventh chord such as G7 (G B D F), the seventh, F, is a
dissonance, and it **falls a step** into the next chord: F to E in G7
to C. The textbook exceptions are not marked:

- the next chord has the same note, and the seventh holds it;
- a **suspension**: the seventh is held into the next chord and falls a
  little later;
- a short **passing** or **neighbour** note that comes and goes by step;
- **V⁴₃ to I⁶**, where the seventh may rise while the bass rises a
  tenth below it.

### What it doesn't check

Hidden fifths and octaves (two parts moving the same way into a fifth
or an octave), the spacing between parts, parts crossing, and which
note of a chord is doubled are left to you. And these are the rules of
one style, the hymns and chorales of the 1700s and the harmony
exercises built on them, not of all music: jazz, pop and Debussy move
in parallel fifths on purpose.

The band keeps the first rule where it holds long chords, as a choir
sings them: in styles such as **Hymn**, **Romanesca** or **Ambient**,
each chord takes, of the voicings nearest the last one, the first that
moves no two parts, the bass among them, in parallel fifths or octaves
or in fifths or octaves by contrary motion, when there is one. From
C E G over C, D minor comes as A D F, the upper parts going down while
the bass goes up.
Styles that play their chords in rhythm take the nearest voicing, as a
pianist's hands do.

Where the key is a **twelve-tone row**, the check uses the rules of
twelve-tone writing instead, and a Row line shows the forms of the row
it reads in the notes: see [Beyond keys: sets, rows and
micromodes](sets-rows-micromodes.md#twelve-tone-rows).

## How each part moves

The check is a teacher's red pen: it says nothing when the parts move
well. To see every move, good or not, point at a chord. The line over
the chord names lists each part from the top down with where it goes,
and then the motion of the outer parts. Pointing at G7 in the exercise:

> Lineup Osc: top A4→F4 · 2nd F4→D4 · 3rd C4→B3 · lowest F3→G3; outer
> parts in contrary motion

Its tooltip says each move in words, with the interval spelled in the
key, "down a major third from A4 to F4, a skip". How far a part moves
has a name:

| Move | Interval | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **held** | the same note | G3 to G3 |
| **step** | a second | F4 to E4 |
| **skip** | a third | B3 to G3 |
| **leap** | a fourth or more | G2 to C3 |

And two parts move together in one of four ways:

| Motion | The two parts | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **parallel** | move the same way, keeping their interval | thirds to thirds |
| **similar** | move the same way, to another interval | a sixth to a fifth, both down |
| **contrary** | move opposite ways | the bass up, the top down |
| **oblique** | one moves, the other holds | a held note under a moving one |

Good part writing moves the inner parts mostly by step or holds them,
lets the bass leap from root to root, and keeps the top part and the
bass in contrary or oblique motion, because the ear follows those two
most. Parallel thirds and sixths are welcome anywhere: they sound sweet
and keep the two lines apart.
