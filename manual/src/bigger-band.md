# A bigger band and the horns

A piano, a bass and drums are a jazz trio, and the rhythm section of
most bands. But a rock band without its guitar, or a tango without its
violins, doesn't sound like itself. Many styles bring more players: a
**guitar**, **strings** or an **organ**, each playing the way that music
uses it, and any style can add a **horn section**.

## The Band menu

**🎷 Band**, at the top left of the chord sheet, chooses who plays and
with which sounds. What they play, the drums and the chord and bass
parts, comes from the Library (see [Styles and the
Library](styles.md)); this menu leaves it alone.

![The Band menu of a bossa nova: Instrument lineup, then Piano, Bass, Drums and Guitar ticked with their instruments, Horn section, Soloist (experimental), Another solo, and No band](images/band-menu.png)

- **Instrument lineup** ▸ puts a whole new set of instruments in the
  band, keeping the rhythm, the chords and the timing: **Grand piano +
  acoustic bass**, **Electric piano + synth bass**, **Piano, acoustic
  guitar + strings**, **Piano, electric guitar + organ**, **Piano +
  strings**, **Piano + nylon guitar**, **Accordion + acoustic bass**,
  or **Horns, timpani + strings**.
- One line for each **player** in the band: **Piano**, **Bass**,
  **Drums**, **Guitar**, **Strings**, **Organ**, **Soloist** and
  **Horns**, as the band has them. Untick one to leave it out of the
  band, and click its instrument to give it another sound, from the 128
  of General MIDI (see [Sounds](sounds.md)). The drums have a channel
  for each drum, so their sounds are chosen in the channel rack.
- **Horn section** adds horns to any style (see below).
- **Soloist (experimental)** and **Another solo**: see [The
  soloist](soloist.md).
- **No band** takes the band away. The chords stay on the sheet, and a
  style from the Library brings a band back.

Each player has its own track in the playlist and its own channel in the
channel rack, with the instrument the style chose.

Switch to a style without a guitar and its track and channel go away;
**Ctrl+Z** brings them back. In the chord sheet's **You play** row, a
player who doesn't play yet is greyed out.

## How the guitar plays

A pianist can play any notes of a chord. A guitarist has six strings,
tuned E A D G B E from the lowest, and four fingers to press them
against the frets. So a guitar chord is a **shape**: a fret, or nothing,
for each string, written in chord books from the low E string, with `x`
for a string not played and `0` for an open one. The lowest string that
sounds must be the chord's bass: the C shape leaves out the low E
string, because an E there would make it C/E.

The guitar in Ferrous Lineup finds its shapes the way a guitarist learns
them, by trying the frets in reach and keeping the easiest: the open
chords of the chord books (C `x32010`, G `320003`, D `xx0232`, Am
`x02210`, G7 `320001`), **barre chords** in other keys (F `133211`, one
finger flat across the first fret), and a shape for every chord in every
key.

It strums them as a guitarist does: a **down-stroke** sweeps the strings
from low to high, a few milliseconds apart, and the lighter
**up-stroke** between the beats comes back over the top four. What it
plays depends on the style:

- **Strummed chords** (pop, folk, country): the whole shape in the
  style's rhythm.
- **Power chords** (rock, punk, metal): only the root, its fifth and the
  octave, `A5`. Distortion turns a third into a sour buzz and a fifth
  into a bigger sound, so rock guitarists leave the third to the bass
  and the singer.
- **Four to the bar** (swing): three-note chords, root, third and
  seventh, struck on every beat, as Freddie Green kept time in Count
  Basie's band.
- **The boogie shuffle** (blues, rock and roll): two strings, the root
  with its fifth, then with its sixth: A and E, A and E, A and F♯, A and
  F♯.
- **The chop** (reggae, soul, Motown, bluegrass): a short chord on the
  top four strings on beats 2 and 4, with the snare. Country calls it the
  "chick" of "boom-chick". In **ska** it plays between all the beats.
- **Scratching** (funk, disco): sixteenth notes on a muted guitar, a few
  accented, most only a percussive "chk".

## Strings and organ

**Strings** hold each chord as long as it lasts, an octave above the
piano, softly: a **pad**, a cushion of sound under the band. Pop uses
them to lift the chorus, so in Pop beat and Motown they only play in
variation b (see [Song form and sections](song-form.md)).

The **organ** holds chords too, as a church organ in a chorale or a
Hammond in a slow blues. In reggae it plays the **bubble**: short chords
between the beats, low down, and an octave higher on beats 2 and 4 with
the guitar's chop. In Rock beat it waits for variation b, the way an
organ fills out a chorus.

## The horn section

Soul and funk records have one, and so do big bands, salsa orchestras,
ska bands and the brass bands of the Balkans: trumpets, saxophones and
trombones playing the chords together. Tick **Horn section** in the Band
menu, and a **Horns** track comes in with the band, playing the chord
sheet in a part that suits the style. Point at **Horn section** to read
what the horns will play in the style you have; untick it to send them
home.

| Style | What the horns play |
|---|---|
| Ballads (Pop ballad, Jazz ballad, Slow blues, Neo soul, R&B, Bossa nova, Bolero…) | long notes held under the band |
| Swing, shuffle, blues, rock and roll | big-band kicks on beat 1 and just before beat 3, the Charleston rhythm |
| Pop, rock, soul, hip hop, Latin, second line | short punches in 3 + 3 + 2, the tresillo |
| Funk | tight stabs in sixteenth notes |
| House and other electronic styles | syncopated stabs |
| Reggaeton, moombahton | short chords in the dembow's 3 + 3 + 2 |
| Ska | short chords between the beats |
| March, waltz, polka, Balkan dances | afterbeats, the "pah" of a brass band's oom-pah |
| Tango, folk, classical, reggae, other styles in 3/4 or 6/8, styles without drums | long notes |
| Other styles in 5/4, 7/8 and other odd meters | a punch on each beat the bar is felt in |

The **3 + 3 + 2** rhythm splits the eight eighth notes of a 4/4 bar into
groups of three, three and two, with an accent at the start of each: on
beat 1, on the "and" of 2, and on beat 4. It is the **tresillo** of Cuban
music, and it is everywhere in pop and soul horn lines. The
**afterbeats** are the off-beats of a brass band: in a march the tuba
plays "oom" on the beat and the horns "pah" between.

The horns play **Brass Section** in most styles, **French Horn** in
classical ones and **Synth Brass 1** in electronic ones; click the
**Horns** line in the Band menu to choose another sound.

### Close harmony

Horns playing chords together voice them in **close position**: the
notes of the chord as near together as they go, inside an octave, under
the highest horn, the **lead**. That is how arrangers make three or four
horns sound like one big instrument.

- There are as many horns as the piano's chord has different notes, up
  to four.
- A chord with more than four notes, such as C13 (C E G B♭ D A), leaves
  out its fifth first and then its root, which the bass plays anyway. The
  horns keep what makes the chord sound as it does: E, B♭, D, A.
- The lead stays between F4 and F5, where a trumpet section sounds
  bright without straining.
- From one chord to the next, each horn goes to the nearest note it can.
  C to A♭ is E4 G4 C5 to E♭4 A♭4 C5: two horns move a semitone, and the
  lead holds C. From G7 to C, B rises to C and F falls to E, as the
  leading tone and the seventh of a dominant do.
- The horns avoid **parallel fifths**: two horns a fifth apart that move
  to another fifth sound like one horn, and a line is lost.

With **Voice leading** ticked in the piano roll you can watch each horn
move (see [Voice leading](voice-leading.md)).
