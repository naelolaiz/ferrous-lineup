# Lead sheets, also for B♭, E♭ and F instruments

A **lead sheet** is what a band reads from: one staff with the tune
(the "lead"), the chords written above it as chord symbols, and the
song's key, time signature, sections and repeats. The bass line, the
drums and how the piano voices its chords are left for the players to
make up. Jazz, pop and folk musicians carry whole books of them.

Ferrous Lineup writes one from your song: the tune is the notes of one
channel, and the chords are those of the chord sheet.

## The Lead sheet window

**View > Lead sheet** opens it. The sheet is laid out on pages as it
will print, and the bar that plays lights up while the song plays.

![The Lead sheet window on the demo I Got Rhythm, written for B♭ instruments: "B♭ instruments" at the top left of the page, the treble clef with no key signature, ♩ = 176, and the chords Am, E7, Am, Dm, then Am6, F7, Am, F, Am6, Fm6 over the tune; the bar numbers 5, 9, 13 and 17 start the lines](images/lead-sheet.png)

Along the top:

- **Melody**: the channel whose notes are the tune. **Auto** takes the
  channel called Lead, which every song demo has, or else the channel
  whose notes are highest. Pick another for another part, the bass for
  a bass player. **None: chords only** writes a slash for every beat
  under the chord symbols, as a rhythm section reads its part.
- **Clef**: **Auto** writes a low tune in the bass clef and the others
  in the treble clef; **Treble** and **Bass** choose one.
- **For**: who reads it (see below).
- **Paper**: **A4**, used almost everywhere, or **Letter**, in the USA
  and Canada.
- **−** and **+** zoom the pages.
- **Export PDF…** saves the sheet as a PDF file.
- **Print…** opens the PDF in your PDF viewer, to print it from there
  with your printer's own settings.

The window only reads the song: nothing in the song changes. The paper,
the zoom and the choice under **For** are remembered.

## Reading a lead sheet

On the first line, from the left:

- the **clef**: the treble clef curls around the G line, the second from
  the bottom; the bass clef's two dots sit around the F line;
- the **key signature**: the sharps or flats of the key, so they need
  not be written on every note. Two flats, B♭ and E♭, are B♭ major or G
  minor; a note outside the key gets its own sharp, flat or natural,
  which lasts until the bar line;
- the **time signature**, only on the first line and where it changes;
- the **tempo**, as in "♩ = 176", 176 quarter notes a minute.

Above the staff, each **chord symbol** stands where its chord starts,
and lasts until the next one. The small number at the start of each line
is the **bar number** of its first bar, so a band can say "from bar
21". Further on, a lead sheet can have:

- **rehearsal marks**, the name of a section in a box where it starts;
  each section starts a new line, so the form of the song shows at a
  glance;
- **repeat signs**, a thick and a thin bar line with two dots, around a
  section played twice, and "×3" when it is played three times or more;
- a **double bar line** before a new section and before a change of
  key, where the new key signature is written;
- the **final bar line**, thin and thick, at the end.

The app chooses how each note is written as an engraver would: a note
of the key as the key spells it, a note of the chord as the chord spells
it (G♯ over E7 in C major, A♭ over Fm), and a note that moves a semitone
by the way it goes (F♯ going up to G, B♭ going down to A). Notes are
split and tied so that the beats of the bar can be seen.

## For B♭, E♭ and F instruments

Ask a pianist and a trumpet player to play the first note of a song in
C together, from the same sheet, and they won't agree: the pianist
plays C, and the trumpet, reading the same C, sounds the B♭ a tone
lower. The trumpet is a **transposing instrument**. Wind instruments
come in families of different sizes, and on all the saxophones, for
instance, the same fingers play the note written C. So music for them is
written for the fingers, and each instrument is named after the note
that sounds when it plays a written C: a trumpet **in B♭**, an alto
saxophone **in E♭**, a horn **in F**. Music written as it sounds is at
**concert pitch**.

| For | Instruments | The part is written |
|---|---|---|
| **Concert pitch** | piano, guitar, bass, voice, flute, violin, trombone | as it sounds |
| **Bb instruments** | trumpet, cornet, flugelhorn, clarinet, soprano saxophone | a major second higher |
| | tenor saxophone | a major ninth higher |
| **Eb instruments** | alto saxophone | a major sixth higher |
| | baritone saxophone | an octave and a sixth higher |
| **F instruments** | horn, English horn | a perfect fifth higher |

So a song in C is written in D for every B♭ instrument, in A for every
E♭ instrument and in G for every F instrument. Choose one under **For**
and the sheet changes at once: the key signature, the tune and the
chord symbols move together, the top of the first page says who the
part is for, and **Export PDF…** puts it in the file name, as in
"I Got Rhythm (Bb instruments).pdf", so the parts of a song don't
overwrite each other. The kind of key stays, and a key never gets more
than six sharps or flats: a part that would be in C♯ major is written in
D♭ major.

The picture shows why so many jazz tunes are in flat keys: *I Got
Rhythm* is in B♭ major, two flats, and the part for B♭ instruments is in
C major, with no sharps or flats at all. Concert E♭ is C for the alto
saxophone, too.
