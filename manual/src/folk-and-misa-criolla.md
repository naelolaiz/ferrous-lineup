# Folk music of South America, and the Misa Criolla

The folk music of the Andes and the Pampas is in **Songs > Latin
American folklore**. Much of it moves in 6/8 and 3/4 at once, the
**hemiola** explained in [Rhythm and meter](rhythm-and-meter.md#two-and-three):
listen for the bars where the tune stops going in two and goes in three.

## Zambas

The **zamba** is the slow dance of the handkerchiefs from the north of
Argentina, in 6/8. A zamba in a minor key often leans on its relative
major for a couple of bars before V7 brings it home.

- *Zamba de mi esperanza*, by Luis Profili, in F major: the zamba that
  Jorge Cafrune's record made known all over Argentina.
- *Alfonsina y el mar*, by Ariel Ramírez and Félix Luna, written for
  Mercedes Sosa about the poet Alfonsina Storni, here for a choir in
  four parts in C minor. Each verse turns to the relative major, E♭,
  through B♭7 and B♭ augmented, whose F♯ rises to G; the chorus ends on
  D major and G7, where the A and B of the melodic minor climb to the
  tonic.
- *Balderrama*, by Cuchi Leguizamón with words by Manuel J. Castilla,
  about the all-night bar in Salta where the musicians and poets met.
  It is in E minor, and one turn of its tune, heard six times, falls
  from D through C♯ to B over a G chord: a chromatic passing note.
- *Zamba por vos*, by the Uruguayan singer Alfredo Zitarrosa.

## The chacarera and its cousins

Short pieces written for Ferrous Lineup show the dances of the region,
each with the hemiola its own way. They are good to study, because they
are short and plain:

| Demo | Dance | Listen for |
|---|---|---|
| *Chacarera del patio* | chacarera, Santiago del Estero | the bombo plays 6/8 on its head and 3/4 on its rim; the V7 bar of each verse and the last bar go in three |
| *Gato del arroyo* | gato, the Pampas | nearly all I and V7; the guitar plays on beats 2 and 3 of a bar of 3/4 against the tune's 6/8 |
| *Malambo del fogón* | malambo, the gaucho's dance of stamping boots | I and V7 in running eighths; bars 5 and 6 switch to 3/4 |
| *Chamamé del estero* | chamamé, Corrientes | the accordion; bars ending in a quarter and an eighth pull into 3/4 over the bass's 6/8 |
| *Cueca de la caleta* | cueca, Chile | hand claps between the beats, and a two-bar *remate*, V7 then I, at the end |
| *Marinera del callejón* | marinera, Peru | guitar and cajón; a quick *fuga* in running eighths ends it |
| *Bambuco del páramo* | bambuco, Colombia | nearly every phrase starts an eighth note after the downbeat |

Each dance is also a style in the Library, so the band can play it
under your own chords (see [Styles and the Library](styles.md)).

## The Andes

The **huayno** is the dance of the Andes of Peru, Bolivia and northern
Argentina, in a quick 2/4, written here two bars to one bar of 4/4.
Its tunes often use the minor pentatonic scale (A C D E G in A minor)
and fall to the same cadence again and again, long, long, short.

- *El cóndor pasa*, by Daniel Alomía Robles, from his zarzuela of 1913:
  a slow theme on A minor and its relative major, C, then the *fuga*, a
  fast dance running up and down the same scale, where the drums come
  in.
- *La Quebrada de Humahuaca*, a traditional huayno in E minor: its
  second strain starts on D, the flat seventh, and there is no D♯
  anywhere.
- *Carnavalito de la quebrada*: the carnival dance of the Quebrada de
  Humahuaca, for quena, charango and bombo, on five notes only.
- *Vidala de la quebrada*: the slow song of the north-west over a hand
  drum. Its chords never use a dominant: G and C, ♭VII and ♭III, lead
  home instead.

## The Misa Criolla

Ariel Ramírez's *Misa Criolla* (1964) is a Mass sung in Spanish, each
of its five movements set to a rhythm of Argentine and Andean folklore.
All five are demos:

| Movement | Rhythm | Listen for |
|---|---|---|
| **Kyrie** | vidala-baguala, slow, 3/4 | the choir's bass climbing by semitones; the Christe in A major; the end on A major, a Picardy third |
| **Gloria** | carnavalito, quick, 2/4 | A minor and E major a beat each; a slow *yaraví* in 3/4 in the middle |
| **Credo** | chacarera trunca, quick, 3/4 | A minor and D major, A Dorian's raised sixth; phrases cut off before the third beat |
| **Sanctus** | carnaval cochabambino, Bolivia, quick, 3/4 | A major; the drums coming in one by one; three bars of 4/4 that broaden into a held chord |
| **Agnus Dei** | estilo pampeano, slow, 3/4, no drums | the guitar's free runs; a plagal close, D minor to A minor |

The Kyrie shows in a few bars how much a bass line can do:

![The chord sheet of the demo Misa Criolla: Kyrie, in A harmonic minor and 3/4: bars 3 to 12 read Am E/B, Am/C A7/C#, Dm, F7/Eb, Am/E, Asus4/E, E7#5, Am(add9), G7/D, C, with their numerals under them, i V⁶₄, i⁶ V⁶₅/iv (in yellow), iv, ♭VI⁴₂, i⁶₄, Isus4, V+7, iadd9, ♭VII⁴₃, ♭III](images/kyrie.png)

From bar 3 the bass climbs A, B, C, C♯, D, E♭, E, a step or a semitone
at a time, and each chord is chosen to fit over it. A7 over C♯ is a
**secondary dominant**, the V of D minor, and its C♯ rises to D (see
[Chords from outside the key](outside-the-key.md#secondary-dominants)).
A minor over E, in bar 7, is the **cadential six-four**, which sits on
the dominant's bass and leads to E.

## Elsewhere in Latin America

- **Mexico**: *La bamba* goes round C, F and G7 without stopping, as a
  *son jarocho* does; *La Llorona*, a slow *son* from Oaxaca in D
  minor, walks down the Andalusian cadence, D minor, C, B♭, A7; *Cielito
  lindo* is a mariachi waltz.
- **Venezuela**: *Alma llanera*, a **joropo** in a quick 3/4, the
  arpeggio standing in for the harp and the *cuatro*.
- **Colombia**: *La gata golosa*, a quick **pasillo** in A major.
- **Uruguay**: the **candombe** of Montevideo. *Candombe del barrio*
  moves in 3 + 3 + 2, the tresillo the milonga and the tango lean on too;
  Rubén Rada's *Candombe para Gardel* keeps changing mode, from G major
  to G minor and back. The Library's **Candombe** and **Candombe
  Ansina** play the three drums of a candombe group: the repique opens
  the song with the clave on the drum's wooden shell, and the phrases
  take turns, so no two in a row sound the same. Before every new part,
  a verse into the refrain, the band **breaks**: it strikes the first
  beat of the last bar and stops, and the repique plays the clave on the
  wood alone, leading into the new part.
- **Brazil**: the songs of the gaúchos of Rio Grande do Sul, such as
  *Boi barroso*, and *Luar do sertão*, a *toada* about the moonlight of
  the backlands.
