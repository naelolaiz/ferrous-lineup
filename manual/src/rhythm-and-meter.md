# Rhythm and meter

Harmony is which notes sound; **rhythm** is when. This chapter covers
how music counts time, how the grid of Ferrous Lineup counts it, and
the meters that don't go in twos and fours.

## Beats and bars

Most music has a **beat**, the pulse you tap your foot to. The
[tempo](tempo-meter-key.md#tempo) says how fast it goes. Beats come in
groups, with the first of each group leaned on a little: those groups
are **bars** (or measures), and the **meter** says how they are made.
Count along with almost any pop song and you count **1** 2 3 4, **1** 2
3 4: four beats to the bar, the first the strongest, the third a little
strong too. A waltz counts **1** 2 3.

The **time signature** writes the meter as two numbers: how many notes
to the bar, and which note (see [Time
signatures](tempo-meter-key.md#time-signatures)).

## Note values and steps

Notes are named by their length, each half the one before:

| Note | In 4/4 | Steps | Draw with |
|---|---|---|---|
| whole note | a bar | 16 | **1 bar** |
| half note | two beats | 8 | **2 beats** |
| quarter note | a beat | 4 | **1 beat** |
| eighth note | half a beat | 2 | **2 steps** |
| sixteenth note | a quarter of a beat | 1 | **1 step** |

A **dot** after a note adds half its length again: a dotted quarter is
three eighths, 6 steps; a dotted eighth is three sixteenths, 3 steps.

The piano roll and the channel rack count in **steps**, and a step is a
sixteenth note: a bar of 4/4 has 16, a bar of 3/4 has 12 and a bar of
7/8 has 14. **Time**, in the harmony keys, says how many steps a bar
has, and **Length** in the piano roll draws the lengths in the last
column; drag a note's right end for any other length.

A **triplet** fits three notes in the time of two: three eighths in a
beat instead of two. Their starts fall between the steps of the grid.
Demos and the MIDI files you open keep them exactly where they are
played.

## Two and three

In **2/4**, **3/4** and **4/4**, each beat is a quarter note that splits
into two eighths: count **1** and 2 and 3 and. These are **simple**
meters.

In **6/8**, **9/8** and **12/8**, the beat is a dotted quarter that
splits into three eighths: 6/8 is two beats, counted **1** 2 3 **4** 5 6,
or **1**-and-a **2**-and-a. These are **compound** meters. A slow blues
or a doo-wop ballad is often in 12/8: four beats, each a group of three.

3/4 and 6/8 both have six eighths to the bar, and that is where the
fun starts:

- **3/4**: three beats of two eighths: **1** & **2** & **3** &;
- **6/8**: two beats of three: **1** 2 3 **4** 5 6.

Much music of Spain and Latin America moves between the two, or plays
both at once. The **zamba** and the **chacarera** of Argentina are
written in 6/8, but their bombo drum plays 6/8 on its skin and 3/4 on
its rim at the same time, and the tunes cross from one to the other,
often in the bar before a cadence. That shift is the **hemiola**, the
*sesquiáltera* of Spanish and Latin American music. Listen for it in
the demo *Zamba de mi esperanza*, or try the **Chacarera** style in the
Library. Leonard Bernstein built "America", in *West Side Story*, on
it: a bar of 6/8, then a bar of 3/4, "I like to be in | A-me-ri-ca".

## Strong beats, weak beats and syncopation

The beats of a bar are not equal. In 4/4 the first is the strongest
and the third next. Rock, pop, soul and funk put the snare drum on the
weak beats, **2** and **4**: that is the **backbeat**, the clap of
gospel music, and what makes a song groove.

**Syncopation** is an accent where the meter doesn't expect one: on
the "and" between beats, or a note that starts just before a strong
beat and holds through it. A chord that arrives an eighth note early,
before the bar line, is a **push** or **anticipation**: the band
plays it with **^** on the chord sheet (see [Shots, holds, rests and
pushes](stops.md)). Latin music is built on syncopated patterns that
repeat every bar or two, like the **clave** of Cuban music and the
**tresillo**, three notes spread over eight eighths as 3 + 3 + 2.

**Swing** is the other great rhythmic idea of jazz: the eighth notes of
each beat played long-short, closer to the first and third notes of a
triplet than to two equal halves. See [Swing and feel](swing.md).

## Odd meters

Some meters count their eighth notes in groups of **two and three
mixed**. A group of three is half as long again as a group of two, so
the beats are long and short, and a dance on them seems to limp:
Turkish musicians call these meters **aksak**, "limping".

| Meter | Counted | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 5/4 | 3 + 2 | *Take Five*, by the Dave Brubeck Quartet |
| 5/8 | 2 + 3 | the Bulgarian paidushko |
| 7/8 | 3 + 2 + 2, long, short, short | the Greek kalamatianos, the Macedonian lesnoto |
| 7/8 | 2 + 2 + 3, short, short, long | the Bulgarian rachenitsa |
| 9/8 | 2 + 2 + 2 + 3 | the Bulgarian daichovo, the Turkish zeybek |
| 11/8 | 2 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 2 | the Bulgarian kopanitsa |

The same 7/8 can limp two ways, so Ferrous Lineup writes the groups in
the time signature: **3+2+2/8** is a lesnoto, **2+2+3/8** a rachenitsa.
The drums, the bass and the chords all follow the groups. In the
channel rack, each beat is shaded on its own, so you can see the long
and short beats:

![The channel rack of the demo Ajde Jano, in 3+2+2/8, showing its pattern Verse drums: 14 steps to the bar, the steps of each beat shaded together, 6, 4 and 4. The kick plays on steps 1, 7 and 11, the first step of each beat, and the rim on the eighth notes between them. Time in the harmony keys reads 3+2+2/8, 14 steps a bar.](images/seven-eight.png)

To hear it, open the demo **Ajde Jano** and count along with the kick:
**1** 2 3, **1** 2, **1** 2. To set an odd meter yourself, open
**Time** and choose **Any other**: the top number, the bottom one and
**Beats grouped as**, such as 3 + 2 + 2. The Library's checkbox **In
3+2+2/8** then shows the styles in that meter.

5/4 doesn't always split 3 + 2. *Take Five*'s piano vamp goes **1** 2 3
**4** 5, but the second movement of Tchaikovsky's *Pathétique Symphony*,
a waltz in five, goes **1** 2 **3** 4 5, a short beat cutting in before
each waltz step.

## Meters that change

Some music changes meter as it goes. The *Promenade* from Mussorgsky's
*Pictures at an Exhibition* puts a bar of 5/4 next to a bar of 6/4,
phrased like speech. A Bavarian **zwiefacher**, such as the demo
*Süsserl*, mixes bars of two and three beats in an order the tune
fixes, and the dancers know it by heart. In Ferrous Lineup each bar can
have its own time signature: right-click a bar number in the playlist
and choose under **Time signature from bar** (see [Changing time
signature partway
through](tempo-meter-key.md#changing-time-signature-partway-through)).
